How this works, and its limits

Where does the data come from?

YouGov's Westminster MRP model, published 24 Sept 2026 — fieldwork 23 Aug–18 Sept 2026, 16,758 respondents, independent (commissioned by YouGov itself, not any party).

Read the write-up · Full seat-by-seat data (CSV), including the 95% credible interval (high/low bound) behind every number on this page.

How willing are voters to vote tactically?

YouGov, 23 Sept 2026 — "What is the tactical voting landscape in September 2026?". In a Labour-vs-Reform head-to-head, Labour 41%, Reform 26%. In a Conservative-vs-Reform head-to-head, Conservatives 35%, Reform 20%.

What about the Electoral Calculus poll?

The Electoral Calculus / Find Out Now poll (24 Sept 2026) was commissioned by Restore Britain — the party this tool excludes from recommendations. That's worth knowing before treating its "tactical voting saves Reform 43 seats" framing as neutral, so this site uses the independent YouGov MRP instead.

Is there a cross-check?

PollCheck.co.uk runs a continuously-updated swingometer (not a full MRP) against a rolling 7-poll average. It broadly agrees on which seats are close, though the two models disagree on individual seats by a few points — a reminder that any single seat's rank can shift.

Which party is recommended?

In every constituency, Reform UK is compared with the strongest other party on YouGov's central estimate. Restore Britain and other extreme parties are never recommended. That strongest other party is the one named for a tactical vote.

YouGov groups independents and small parties together as "Others", so an individual independent candidate can't be recommended. Where Others poll strongly (20% or more), the result says so, and it's worth checking who is standing locally.

How is a constituency judged "close"?

A constituency is marked Close only if it passes both of these tests. Both use YouGov's published figures as they are, with no extra modelling, and both work the same way whichever party is ahead.

  1. The gap test: Reform's central estimate and the best-placed alternative's central estimate are within 10 points of each other.
  2. The range test: the two parties' 95% ranges overlap. In other words, the trailing party's highest plausible share is at least as high as the leading party's lowest plausible share.

Example — Close: Broxtowe. Labour 30% (range 23–38%), Reform 24% (range 18–30%). The gap is 6 points, and the ranges overlap.

Example — not close: Great Yarmouth. Reform 32% (range 20–43%), Conservative 18% (range 12–24%). The ranges overlap, but the gap is 14 points, so it fails the gap test.

In the current data, every seat within 10 points also has overlapping ranges, so the gap test is effectively the deciding one. Both are kept so the rule stays robust when the data is updated.

What do the labels mean?
  • Close passes both tests above (324 constituencies). This is where a tactical vote matters most.
  • Reform well ahead means Reform leads and the race isn't close (11 constituencies). The best-placed challenger is still named, but they are a long way behind, so voting for the party you genuinely prefer is also a reasonable choice.
  • Safe from Reform means another party leads Reform comfortably (297 constituencies). No tactical vote is needed.

The gap is the difference in points between Reform and the best-placed alternative, whichever is ahead.
